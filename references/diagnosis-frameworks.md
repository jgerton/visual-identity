# Diagnosis Frameworks Reference

Operational distillation of 5 frameworks used in brand diagnosis. Cut: history, citations, workshop formats.

---

## 1. Dunford Positioning (5 Components)

**Objective:** Identify the specific market context where a product's differentiated value is most obvious to its best-fit customers.

**Usage note:** Run as analytical lenses on existing client materials and research, not as a client interview.

### Component 1: Competitive Alternatives

**Question:** What would customers do if this solution didn't exist?

- This is NOT a competitor list; it's what customers actually consider or currently do
- "Do nothing" is valid (spreadsheets, manual processes, hiring someone)
- Avoid phantom competitors: companies that theoretically compete but never appear in real decisions
- In B2B, ~25% of decisions are lost to "no decision"; position against the status quo too

**Source notes:** Sales call recordings, win/loss data, customer interviews, review sites

### Component 2: Unique Attributes

**Question:** What does this brand have that alternatives do NOT?

- List everything: features, business model, supply chain, process, IP, partnerships, team
- Don't judge value yet; even "complexity" can be valuable in the right context
- Include non-feature differentiators: pricing model, delivery method, community, expertise

**Source notes:** Product pages, founder bios, about pages, customer reviews mentioning what made them switch

### Component 3: Value

**Question:** So what? What can those unique attributes DO for customers?

- Customers care about outcomes, not features; map each attribute to customer-facing value
- Value can come from a combination of attributes, not just individual ones
- Every value claim must trace back to a specific unique attribute; if it can't, it's generic

**Source notes:** Case studies, testimonials, results pages, sales messaging

### Component 4: Best-Fit Customers

**Question:** Who are the customers that care the MOST about this differentiated value?

- Many prospects care somewhat; the target is those who care a lot
- Best-fit customers understand value quickly, purchase faster, and rarely ask for discounts
- Get specific beyond demographics: what situation, trigger, or pain makes them care intensely?

**Source notes:** Customer testimonials, founder stories about "aha" moments, community membership patterns

### Component 5: Market Category

**Question:** What frame of reference makes the value most obvious to best-fit customers?

- Declaring a category sets assumptions about competitors, features, and pricing
- Three styles: head-to-head (need clear differentiators), niche (own a subsegment, best for most), new category (expensive, risky)
- Test: does this category make the unique value obvious, or does it obscure it?
- A niche of an existing category is almost always safer than inventing a new one

**Source notes:** How the brand describes itself in headlines and bios, how customers describe it in reviews

### Quality Criteria

- Every value claim traces to a specific unique attribute (no floaters)
- Best-fit customer definition includes a situational trigger, not just demographics
- Market category highlights strengths rather than burying them
- Alternatives list reflects what customers actually consider, not theoretical competitors

### Business-Type Adaptations

| Type | Competitive Alternatives | Best-Fit Customer Signal |
|------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| SaaS | Other tools, spreadsheets, manual process, hiring | Role + company size + specific pain + trigger event |
| Local service | DIY, different provider type, ignoring the problem | Geography + trigger event (move, renovation, emergency) |
| Content creator | Other creators, free content, books, doing without | Skill level + goal + current frustration |
| Ecommerce | Direct competitors, Amazon, DIY, going without | Lifestyle + values + purchase trigger |
| Community/network | Solo work, other communities, social media scrolling | Identity + aspiration + current isolation point |
| Agency | In-house hire, freelancer, larger agency, doing without | Company stage + internal capacity gap + urgency |

## 2. Neumeier Onlyness Test

**Objective:** Compress a brand's differentiation into a single sentence that only that brand could claim.

**Template:** `The only [category] that [benefit]`

### Extended 6-Question Format

- **WHAT** is the category?
- **HOW** is it different?
- **WHO** are the customers?
- **WHERE** are they located?
- **WHEN** do they need it?
- **WHY** does it matter?

Combine the answers into one sentence. If the sentence requires "and" more than once, the position is not focused enough.

### Quality Criteria

- Passes the swap test: swapping in a competitor's name makes the statement false
- "Only" must be defensible; if five competitors could say the same thing, it fails
- The benefit clause names a specific customer outcome, not a feature
- The category is narrow enough to own, not broad enough to lose
- A competitor should be able to read it and feel tension

### Examples

**Pass:** "The only email tool for founders that kills the draft by letting you write one sentence and send" (specific category, specific benefit, swap fails immediately)

**Fail:** "The only marketing platform that helps businesses grow faster" (category too broad, benefit generic, any competitor could claim it)

### Business-Type Adaptations

- **SaaS:** Onlyness found in workflow, integration pattern, or pricing model, not just features
- **Local service:** Geographic + specialization focus; "the only [service] in [area] that [specific approach]"
- **Content creator:** Creator's perspective + audience identity; the "only" is often the person plus their specific lens
- **Community:** Tribe identity is the product; onlyness is about who belongs and why
- **Ecommerce:** Product sourcing, curation angle, or experience differentiator

## 3. Aaker Brand Personality

**Objective:** Identify the human personality traits a brand projects so voice and visual decisions stay consistent.

### 5 Dimensions

| Dimension | Example Traits |
|-----------|---------------|
| Sincerity | Down-to-earth, honest, wholesome, cheerful |
| Excitement | Daring, spirited, imaginative, up-to-date |
| Competence | Reliable, intelligent, successful, expert |
| Sophistication | Upper-class, charming, glamorous, refined |
| Ruggedness | Outdoorsy, tough, athletic, gritty |

### Usage Notes

- Select a primary dimension and one secondary; more than two dilutes personality
- Translate the dimension into 3-5 specific adjectives (not the dimension name itself)
- Avoid generic defaults: "professional," "friendly," "innovative" appear on 90% of brand briefs and mean nothing
- The final adjective set should make someone say "yes, that's them" when they see the content

### Quality Criteria

- Must pass the Voice Contrast Test: the same message written in the opposite dimension sounds obviously wrong
- Must be visually actionable: a designer can translate the adjectives into type, color, and imagery decisions
- Primary and secondary dimensions create productive tension, not contradiction (e.g., Competence + Excitement works; Sophistication + Ruggedness rarely does)
- No dimension used as a label; always expressed as specific adjectives

### Business-Type Adaptations

- **SaaS:** Common default is Competence; add Excitement or a Sincerity secondary to avoid sounding like every other SaaS
- **Local service:** Sincerity works; fix with specific local texture instead of generic warmth phrases
- **Content creator:** Voice IS the product; document the creator's natural voice quirks and amplify what's already there
- **Ecommerce:** Dimension should reflect who the customer becomes by buying, not what the product does

## 4. Jung Archetypes

**Objective:** Anchor the brand's character and motivational core so voice, story, and visual tone all pull in the same direction.

### 12 Archetypes

| Archetype | Core Desire | Brand Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| Innocent | Safety, simplicity | Dove, Coca-Cola |
| Explorer | Freedom, discovery | Patagonia, REI |
| Sage | Knowledge, truth | Google, BBC |
| Hero | Mastery, courage | Nike, FedEx |
| Outlaw | Liberation, revolution | Harley-Davidson, Virgin |
| Magician | Transformation | Apple, Disney |
| Regular Guy/Gal | Belonging | IKEA, Target |
| Lover | Intimacy, experience | Godiva, Victoria's Secret |
| Jester | Joy, fun | Old Spice, M&M's |
| Caregiver | Service, protection | Johnson & Johnson, TOMS |
| Creator | Innovation, expression | Adobe, LEGO |
| Ruler | Control, stability | Mercedes, Rolex |

### Usage

Select ONE primary archetype. Optionally name one secondary for nuance; if secondary contradicts primary (e.g., Innocent + Outlaw), that tension is a positioning problem, not a feature. Write a one-sentence rationale: "This brand is [archetype] because its customers come to it wanting [core desire], and the brand delivers that through [specific mechanism]."

### Quality Criteria

- Archetype aligns with the audience's aspiration or self-image, not just the brand's self-perception
- The choice produces immediate voice implications (a Jester brand should not write like a Sage)
- Secondary archetype adds dimension without contradicting primary
- The rationale sentence is specific enough that a different archetype would produce a different rationale

## 5. Miller Problem Structure (Simplified)

**Objective:** Map the customer's problem at four levels so messaging addresses both rational and emotional pain, not just surface symptoms.

**Usage note:** Only the problem structure from StoryBrand, not the full BrandScript.

### 4 Levels

**Villain**
The root cause, framed as a force the customer fights. NOT a competitor. NOT an abstract noun like "challenges."
- What it is: a specific force that creates the external problem (complexity, wasted time, broken systems, gatekeeping, inequity)
- What it is NOT: a person, a competitor, or a vague concept like "the modern world"

**External Problem**
The tangible, surface-level issue. What the customer would Google or describe to a friend.
- What it is: the concrete, observable problem ("I can't get my team to follow the process")
- What it is NOT: an internal feeling or a philosophical statement

**Internal Problem**
The feeling the external problem creates. This is the emotional hook.
- What it is: frustration, overwhelm, self-doubt, embarrassment, feeling behind or stuck
- What it is NOT: the external problem restated with emotional language sprinkled on top

**Philosophical Problem**
Why it's wrong that the customer has this problem. Frames the stakes morally.
- What it is: "No one should have to..." or "It's wrong that [group] can't access [thing]"
- What it is NOT: a mission statement or brand value; must name the specific injustice

### Quality Criteria

- The villain is specific enough to put in a sentence without the word "challenges"
- External and internal problems are distinct; a reader can tell them apart immediately
- The philosophical level names a genuine wrong, not a marketing platitude
- All four levels are consistent: they describe the same customer in the same situation

### Examples

**Pass (villain):** "The villain is manual data re-entry: every change made in one system has to be manually copied into three others, and the customer owns that error risk."

**Fail (villain):** "The villain is inefficiency." (Too abstract; any brand could claim it; produces no voice or messaging direction.)

### Business-Type Adaptations

| Type | Villain | Internal Problem |
|------|---------|-----------------|
| SaaS | Broken workflows, data silos, tool sprawl | Frustration, feeling behind, overwhelmed by tools |
| Local service | Unreliable providers, DIY risk, neglect | Embarrassment, stress, feeling like a bad homeowner |
| Content creator / community | Gatekeeping, information overload, isolation | Impostor syndrome, feeling lost, behind peers |
| Ecommerce | Compromise and settling, decision fatigue | Guilt, dissatisfaction, fear of buyer's remorse |
| Agency | Lack of internal expertise, wrong hire, wasted budget | Exposed, out of control, afraid of being blamed |
